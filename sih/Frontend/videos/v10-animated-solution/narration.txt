01|A signal flashes, then disappears. The receiver reaches that frequency too late.
02|The antenna may receive a wide range, but the tuner can inspect only a narrow slice at once. The problem statement says that slice is typically at least ten times narrower than the range to monitor. Our simulation uses two of sixteen bands.
03|A burst outside that slice is invisible at that moment. Software cannot widen the hardware window. It can choose where the window goes next.
04|Each scan gives us evidence. We update what we know, choose the next two bands, retune, and scan again. All six strategies get the same virtual receiver and time budget.
05|Two use designed rules. C T M C keeps a random chance of visiting every band. Index ranks bands by likely activity and by how long they have waited.
06|Two learn from rewards. Bandit weighs a promising band against a new one. Q-learning remembers which choice worked in each situation.
07|Two use neural learning. D Q N estimates the value of each choice. P P O adjusts the probabilities of its scanning choices.
08|We test generated emitter patterns and Alan Turing Institute synthetic radar pulses through the virtual receiver. These are simulations, not field measurements.
09|In held-out Scenario B, fixed sweep sees about eleven percent of active band-time. Index sees thirteen percent. That is a twenty-two percent relative gain with the same narrow window.
10|Both reach about ninety-seven percent of distinct emitters. In a separate test, Bandit sees more active time but fewer emitters. That is a tradeoff.
11|Training times were not logged. The warm scheduler API took six point six milliseconds at the ninety-fifth percentile. The receiver remains narrow.
